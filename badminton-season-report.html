Partnership analysis

Partnerships here are assigned, not chosen: the workbook builds balanced teams from its ratings, so strong players are usually paired with weak ones. That makes a raw partnership record mostly a measure of the two players’ combined strength. To find genuine chemistry, each pair’s results are compared with what the two players’ separate estimates predict against the opponents they actually met. Out of — partnerships, — differs from expectation by more than chance would usually allow — about what chance alone would produce across that many pairs. In short, there is no reliable evidence of special chemistry or clashes; partnership results are explained by individual strength.

Opponent analysis

In doubles each match puts a player against two opponents at once, and every opponent also had a partner, so a head-to-head record is shared credit. Records against the strongest players are poor for everyone simply because those players are strong. The useful question is whether anyone does unusually well or badly against a particular opponent once strength is accounted for. The answer is no: across all pairings with — or more meetings, the largest such deviation is — points per match, within normal match-to-match noise. There are no hidden “bogey” matchups in this data.

Method

Estimate
  1. Adjusted plus-minus (ridge regression). Each match is one equation: team A’s margin = the two A players’ values − the two B players’ values + noise. Solving all — equations together separates each player from partners and opponents. A small ridge penalty (λ = —, chosen by leaving out one day at a time) keeps estimates stable; values sum to zero, so an estimate of one point means a player adds one point per match more than the average of these — players.
  2. Uncertainty. Whole session days were resampled with replacement — times and the model refitted each time; the middle — of refits gives each interval and the rank probabilities.
  3. Form and change. A recency-weighted fit (weights halve every — session days) estimates current level. Separate fits of the first and last — days estimate change. Daily adjusted performance — each day’s margins minus what partners and opponents predict — feeds the trend and consistency figures.
  4. Elo. Each player starts at —. A team’s rating is the average of its two players; after each match both winners gain and both losers lose — × (result − expected), scaled up for wider margins. Elo depends on match order and needs time to settle, so it is a progression view, not the main estimate.
  5. Expectations. A predicted margin and the model’s leftover spread (— points) give each team’s chance of winning. Predictions used for expected wins come from fits that leave out the day being predicted.

How well it works, and its limits

Estimate
  • Validation. Predicting each day from the other —, the model’s average margin error is — points against — for a no-information guess, and it names the winner — of the time. It explains about — of the variation in margins; the rest is ordinary match-to-match variation. Elo picked — of winners from W1 D4 on.
  • The workbook’s own rating uses the same kind of fit and reports a similar error, and its order now matches this model’s. Two differences: it puts Pratap and Bhanu level at 1, while the model puts them — points per match apart; and its note that Jothi Sai (—) has passed Anto (—) is not reproduced — this model has Anto ahead in — of resamples, and so does the recency-weighted fit.

  • Small samples. Players have —–— matches. Season estimates are good to about — points; pair and head-to-head figures are much less certain.
